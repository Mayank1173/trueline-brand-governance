# Trueline Research and Feedback Plan

Status: planning document only. No research has been collected yet. All numbers in this plan are targets, not results. Do not add participant details, quotes, derived rates, or findings until real data exists.

## 1. Purpose and research questions

The purpose of this research is to learn how teams review content before publication, what makes a review finding trustworthy, and what would stop a team from using a tool like Trueline. The research should feed product decisions, not decorate them.

### Research targets

| Activity | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Round 1 interviews | 6 to 10 interviews — target, not achieved | Planned |
| Round 1 survey | 30 or more responses — target, not achieved | Planned |
| Round 2 tester feedback | 5 or more testers — target, not achieved | Planned |

### Research questions

| ID | Research question |
|---|---|
| RQ-01 | How do teams create, review, and approve content before publication today? |
| RQ-02 | Where do brand rules and approved facts live, and how do people know which version is current? |
| RQ-03 | How do people check claims, prices, offers, and product facts before publishing? |
| RQ-04 | What makes a review finding trustworthy and actionable? |
| RQ-05 | How do people prefer to apply suggested fixes while preserving the author’s meaning? |
| RQ-06 | Who should approve content, and what should they see before deciding? |
| RQ-07 | What revision and audit records do teams need? |
| RQ-08 | What would stop a team from adopting a tool like Trueline? |

### How to recruit

- Use team contacts, small-business networks, marketing communities, and student teams.
- Ask short screening questions about the person’s role and whether they create, review, or approve content.

## 3. Consent and privacy

Every interview, survey, and test session must follow these rules:

- Participation is voluntary.
- Participants can skip any question.
- Participants can stop at any time.
- The consent text must explain the study purpose, voluntary participation, how responses will be used, and how findings will be reported.
- Collect no names, contact details or other identifying information unless necessary; if contact details are needed for follow-up, keep them in a separate file or form from the responses.
- Do not collect confidential company data, customer data, unpublished pricing, or personal data.
- Use participant codes instead of names.
- Remove company names and identifying details from notes.
- Ask permission before recording. If recording is not allowed, take notes only.
- Raw notes and survey data are accessible only to the people who need them.
- Keep raw notes in a private location agreed with the team.
- Share only anonymised summaries.
- No identifiable quotes or personal details are published without permission.
- Raw interview notes are kept until the final event round is complete, then deleted (see section 8).
- Explain that the purpose is product learning, not performance evaluation.

## 4. Interview guide

Ask these questions in a neutral tone. Ask for recent examples. Do not suggest the answer you want.

1. Walk me through how content gets created, reviewed, and approved in your team today.
2. What usually goes wrong during that process, and what happens next?
3. How does feedback usually reach the person who wrote the draft?
4. When you see a review comment, how do you decide whether to trust it?
5. What does a useful explanation of a brand issue look like to you?
6. What would you never want an AI system to do in a content review?
7. How do you handle prices, offers, or product facts that may be outdated?
8. Who makes the final approval decision, and what do they need to see before deciding?

### Follow-up probes

- Can you give me a recent example?
- What happened next?
- What made that easy or difficult?
- What did you do instead?
- Who else was involved?
- What would have helped in that moment?

1. Clean and anonymise notes within one day.
2. Remove names, company names, customer data, and confidential details.
3. Split notes into small notes with one idea each.
4. Tag each note as `Observation`, `Assumption`, or `Recommendation`.
5. Link each note to a research question and, where possible, an FR ID.
6. Group similar notes into themes.
7. Count the number of distinct participants who raised each theme. Do not count repeated mentions by the same person twice.
8. Record evidence strength: single signal, emerging pattern, or repeated pattern.
9. Look for contradictions and negative cases.
10. Write a finding only when it includes the participant count and the source method.
11. Keep survey counts separate from interview counts.
12. Mark every result as directional while the sample is small.

**Hard rule:** no theme is reported without the count of participants who raised it. Do not write “users want” or “teams need” without that count.

## 13. Risks and how to reduce them

| Risk | Why it matters | How to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| Leading questions | Participants may agree with the team’s assumptions | Use neutral open questions, ask for recent examples, and pilot the guide |
| Small sample | Results may not represent a wider group | Report source counts, not derived rates; label results as directional |
| Friendly-participant bias | Friends and students may be too positive | Label practice participants, recruit outside the team, and ask about blockers |
| Social-desirability bias | People may describe ideal behaviour rather than real behaviour | Ask what happened in a recent real case |
| Analysis bias | The team may notice only supportive notes | Tag observations first, include contradictions, and ask a second person to review themes |
| Confidentiality breach | Participants may share company data | Use the consent script, remove names, and delete raw notes after the final event round, using the deletion checkpoint in section 8 |
| Low survey response | The target may not be reached | Keep the survey short, use several channels, and report the real count |
| Demo bias | Testers may react to the presenter rather than the product | Use task-based questions and label `MOCKUP` or `SIMULATED` sessions clearly |
| Overclaiming | Targets may be mistaken for results | Keep the results section empty, show participant counts, and use target labels |
| Mixing practice and target-user notes | Practice feedback may look like customer evidence | Use a `PRACTICE` label and report the groups separately |
